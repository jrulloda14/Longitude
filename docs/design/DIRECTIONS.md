# Design directions

Three directions were explored for the same interaction model. **A · Instrument** is the system above.

## A · Instrument (chosen)

Graphite ground, warm ivory ink, one signal-orange hand. The dial reads like a watch face: hairline ticks, tabular numerals, a date window at 3 and an AM/PM window at 9, and a thin sky ring that shows real daylight for the reference city. Rows are compact readouts, not cards.

- **Why it wins.** The clock is the hero because it *looks* like the thing you operate, and the single accent maps to exactly one idea — "the time you've picked" — so the eye finds it instantly in the dial, rows, timeline and summary. Dark-first neutral surfaces sit quietly beside Teams and Outlook, and the warm ink keeps it from feeling like an IDE.
- **Risks handled.** Orange next to the amber day palette could blur: day states use a separate sage / sand / slate family, and verdict levels add glyphs and words.
- **Ports to macOS** by swapping caption buttons for traffic lights; nothing in the identity is Windows-specific.

## B · Almanac (not chosen)

Light-first, cream paper and ink, a serif for numerals, 24-hour dial with a printed sunrise/sunset band, ruled rows like an almanac table.

- Strong personality and great in light mode, but a serif readout fights tabular alignment at 20px, the paper metaphor goes muddy in dark mode (the stated primary), and a 24-hour dial makes the common "set 3:30" gesture less precise than a 12-hour face.

## C · Sky ring (not chosen)

The whole dial is a 24-hour sky gradient (night → dawn → day → dusk); cities are dots orbiting the ring; dragging spins the sky.

- Memorable and beautiful in a screenshot, but the gradient becomes decoration that competes with text, dots overlap when two cities share an offset, and reading exact minutes off an orbit is hard. Its best idea — real daylight on the dial — survives in A as the thin sky ring.
